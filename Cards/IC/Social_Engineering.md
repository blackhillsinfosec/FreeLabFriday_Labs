# Social Engineering

Social engineering is when an attacker targets **people instead of systems**. Instead of breaking technical defenses directly, they convince someone to help them - often without the victim realizing it.

The goal is usually the same as any other attack: gain access to credentials, sensitive information, or internal systems. What makes social engineering dangerous is that it exploits trust, urgency, and normal human behavior.

---

## How Social Engineering Usually Works

Most social engineering attacks follow a simple pattern:

- The attacker gathers information about a target (company, team, or person)
- Builds a believable story or identity
- Creates pressure or trust to influence decisions
- Tricks the victim into revealing information or performing an action
- Uses that access to bypass technical controls

Common examples include phishing emails, fake IT support calls, impersonation, and fraudulent requests for urgent actions.

---

## Why It Works

People naturally want to be helpful and efficient. Attackers take advantage of this by using:

- Authority (“I’m from IT” or “I’m your manager”)
- Urgency (“This needs to be done now”)
- Fear (“Your account will be disabled”)
- Curiosity or rewards
- Familiarity and trust

Even well-secured environments can be compromised if a user is convinced to take the wrong action.

---

## How Attackers Prepare

Before interacting with a victim, attackers often collect information through open sources:

- Public social media profiles
- Company websites
- Job postings
- Breach data or leaked credentials
- Public documents and metadata

This research helps them make messages feel realistic and targeted.

---

## Detecting Social Engineering Activity

Social engineering often leaves indirect traces rather than obvious malware alerts. Detection usually involves:

- Endpoint security protection analysis
- Network threat hunting
- User and Entity Behavior Analytics (UEBA)
- Cloud event log analysis

Behavioral anomalies - such as unusual logins, strange data access, or unexpected account actions - are common indicators.
